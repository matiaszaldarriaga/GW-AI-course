"GOAL
====
The gravitational wave background seen by a pulsar timing array is made by a
finite number of supermassive black hole binaries sitting at particular places
on the sky. Because there are finitely many of them, the power on the sky is
not uniform.

Work out the angular power spectrum of that sky exactly.

Concretely: let the source-power sky be

    P(n) = sum_a q_a delta^2(n, n_a)

for N sources at directions n_a carrying power q_a. Derive C_l^(P) in terms of
the fractions p_a = q_a / sum_b q_b of the total power that each source
carries. Then say what it becomes when one source dominates, and what it
becomes when all N sources are equal.

Check the result numerically with `healpy`: build the sky, run `anafast`, and
compare against your formula. Say how well they agree and at which l the
comparison stops being meaningful, and why.

This is the power sky. It is not the same object as the angular power spectrum
of the pulsar redshift map, whose l-space shape gives the Hellings-Downs curve.
If you use the symbol C_l, say every time which of the two you mean.

gravitational wave background, for what C_l/C_0 means observationally and how

The standard scientific python stack, plus `healpy`.

   reproduce the measured background amplitude, spanning a range that covers

   spectrum, and searching directly for the brightest source. Give the
   significance of each in terms of the signal-to-noise ratio of the background
